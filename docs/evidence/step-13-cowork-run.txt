24 lines, 6.34s of summed handler time across a 447s run.

`started at` is the completion timestamp minus the handler's own latency. **Every call starts
after the previous one has returned.** There is not one overlapping pair in the run. Compare
the harness at concurrency 8, where the whole point is that wall clock lands below summed
handler time; here they are the same number laid end to end.

This is the artifact step 7's evidence wished it had. That step had to qualify its no-fan-out
claim because it rested on the run's narration rather than a per-call log (commit 9ff1ff7).
This one is measured, and it happens to measure the opposite of what was hoped for, which is
the reason for measuring it.

**It is not even leg-shaped.** Step 4a describes a leg as two calls for one engagement:
get_engagement_burn and get_financials, paired, so the leg is the unit an agent could own. The
run did ten burns and then ten financials -- tool-major, not engagement-major. So it did not
read 4a and decline to parallelise; it organised the work along the other axis entirely, on
which "one subagent per engagement" does not map onto anything. The instruction did not take.


================================================================================
AND IT WOULD HAVE SAVED ALMOST NOTHING
================================================================================

This is the more useful half, and it was not visible before this run.

    total run, first call to finalize             447.4s
    summed handler time, all 24 calls               6.3s     1.4% of the run
    the detail leg, first burn to last financial   19.7s
    summed handler time inside the detail leg       2.1s    10.7% of the leg

The twenty detail calls average 105ms each and sit about 700-1300ms apart. The gaps are model
turnaround, not database time. Inside the window step 13 set out to compress, nine parts in
ten are the model deciding what to call next.

So a perfect eight-way fan-out of that leg saves roughly 1.8 seconds out of 447 -- **0.4% of
the run.** The 348-second gap between the last financial and the first publish_pack, which is
classify.py, the two builders and the model's own writing, is on its own 175 times larger than
everything fan-out could win.

The harness numbers are not wrong and this does not retract them. 1.64x local and 1.45x on
Aurora are real, and they are what the fan-out is worth *to the tool-call portion of a run*.
This run measures how large that portion is when a model is driving instead of a script, and
the answer is 1.4%. Both facts are needed to say anything true about the step:

    the fan-out works, is safe, and is worth 1.45x of the 1.4% of a Cowork run that is
    spent talking to the database

Which reframes the cut-order line in the build plan. Step 13 sits first to be cut, and that
was the right call for a better reason than the one written down: not because it is the least
finished, but because on the path that actually ships it optimises the wrong thing. Worth
knowing that it is safe and cheap to turn on the day a portfolio has 200 engagements rather
than 18, and worth not believing it does anything today.

the step started, showing up in the one place it was expected to, and it confirms the
mechanism rather than contradicting anything.

================================================================================
WHAT WOULD HAVE TO CHANGE
================================================================================

Not recorded as a to-do list -- the measurement above says the payoff is 0.4% -- but the run
narrows why the instruction did not take, and that is worth having written down.

Step 4a sits inside step 4, after the six trigger lists, as prose. Everything around it in
that section is about *which* engagements to examine, and it is the only paragraph about *how*
to make the calls. A model reading the section for its filter can take the whole thing as
answered once the union is written out. The tool-major ordering it chose is the natural shape
if you read step 4 as "collect the union, then get their burn, then get their financials",
which is what the rest of the section reads like.

The cheap experiments, in order of how much they would tell us: make 4a its own numbered step
rather than a sub-step of the selection rule; state the leg as a two-call pair before
mentioning subagents at all, since the pairing is the part it got wrong and the parallelism is
downstream of it; and only then a prompt that names parallelism, which would answer whether
the Skill can carry it or only the prompt can.

None of these were tried. Tuning the prompt until it wins would produce a number about the
prompt rather than about the Skill, and the runbook said so before the run for exactly this
reason.
